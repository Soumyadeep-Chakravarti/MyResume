# world.md

# World Spec — First Shipping World

This file is the **physical world spec** for the project.

Not the emotional pitch.
Not the architecture constitution.
Not the content model.

This is the document that answers:

* **What is the actual place the user moves through?**
* **What is physically in it?**
* **How does scrolling map to movement through it?**
* **What parts of the world are permanent, and what parts are content carriers?**
* **What are the first-ship constraints so I don’t disappear into infinite environment design?**

---

# 1. What this world actually is

The world is a **coastal cliffside road system carved into a Japanese-inspired ruin**.

That sentence matters because it cuts away a lot of bad directions.

This is **not**:

* a floating abstract sci-fi room
* a generic “3D portfolio landscape”
* a temple exploration game
* a field-research simulator
* a city builder
* a giant lore-heavy narrative world

It is a **place you move through**. A harsh, engineered road network cut into a massive cliff face near the sea, with old structures embedded into the rock and modern insertions grafted onto them over time.

The cliff is the constant.
The roads are the traversal system.
The structures are environmental anchors.
The content lives in **designed interaction points along the route**, not in random floating UI.

So the world should feel like:

* **coastal**
* **elevated**
* **weathered**
* **engineered into rock**
* **part ruin, part living infrastructure**
* **Japanese in architectural influence, not cosplay**
* **cinematic at a distance, precise up close**

The user is not “reading a website.”
They are **moving through a place that happens to contain me**.

---

# 2. The core spatial fantasy

The fantasy is simple:

> You enter from the upper cliff edge and descend through a sequence of roads, cuts, overlooks, tunnels, platforms, and attached structures built into the rock.

That is the first shipping fantasy.

Not “explore an entire world.”
Not “walk anywhere.”
Not “open world.”

It is a **directed descent** through a carefully staged environment.

That matters because it gives me control over:

* pacing
* reveals
* sightlines
* camera composition
* when content appears
* when the world opens up
* when the world narrows and becomes intimate

The scroll journey is basically a **cinematic mountain road / cliff descent** with detours into important spaces.

---

# 3. World pillars

The world has 5 non-negotiable pillars.

## 3.1 The cliff is the primary object

The cliff is not background art.
It is the main body of the world.

Everything is attached to it, cut into it, suspended from it, or built on top of it.

The cliff gives me:

* verticality
* scale
* weight
* geology
* a reason for roads, tunnels, drainage, overlooks, and retaining structures
* a natural way to hide and reveal parts of the world

If I ever feel tempted to fill space with random props or floating setpieces, the answer should usually be:
**put it into the cliff, onto the cliff, or along the road.**

---

## 3.2 The road is the experience spine

The road system is the real navigation structure.

Not a page tree.
Not section anchors pretending to be a page.
Not disconnected “rooms.”

The roads do the work of:

* progression
* camera direction
* pacing
* framing the world
* carrying the user from one major moment to the next

The first ship should treat the road as the **main experience spine**.

That means the camera journey should feel like:

* descending a winding cliff road
* crossing a narrow elevated section
* passing into a tunnel / covered cut
* emerging into a wider overlook
* entering a more built-up zone where content becomes denser

The world can branch visually later, but the **first ship should stay disciplined and mostly linear**.

---

## 3.3 Structures are anchors, not the whole point

There **are** structures in this world, but they are not the main fantasy.

I do not want to build a “temple portfolio” where every interesting thing is a building facade.

The structures exist to do 4 jobs:

1. **Scale anchors**
   They help the cliff feel inhabited and engineered.

2. **Transition anchors**
   They make the route feel like it passes through real spaces instead of pure terrain.

3. **Content docking points**
   Some structures host panels, acrylic cards, screens, light wells, or reveal points.

4. **Silhouette + memory**
   They make the world recognizable from a distance.

So yes, there are buildings, pavilions, retaining walls, gate-like frames, cable runs, drainage systems, service platforms, tunnel mouths, and cliffside installations.

But the user should not come away thinking:
“cool building showcase.”

They should come away thinking:
“that whole place felt real.”

---

## 3.4 Water and weather are not decoration

Water matters in this world.

Rain matters.
Wet surfaces matter.
Drainage matters.
Mist matters.
Refraction matters.
Specular highlights matter.
Thunder matters.

This is one of the biggest reasons I’m even doing this world in the first place.

I want:

* wet cliff faces
* runoff channels
* puddles and reflective patches
* dripping overhangs
* mist layers near the sea
* occasional water sheeting over stone
* acrylic / glass / lit surfaces that react beautifully to moisture and light

Water is one of the main reasons the world can feel alive without stuffing it full of moving nonsense.

So in this project, water and weather are part of the world’s identity, not a post-processing garnish.

---

## 3.5 The world must stay believable even when stylized

I do not care about realism in the literal sense.
I care about **physical conviction**.

The world can be stylized.
The lighting can be dramatic.
The composition can be theatrical.
The colors can be pushed.

But the place still has to obey believable logic:

* roads need support
* water needs somewhere to go
* structures need anchoring
* tunnels need openings and exits
* railings / barriers / retaining walls need to exist where needed
* if a panel or acrylic card is mounted somewhere, it should feel mounted on purpose
* if light comes from somewhere, there should be a reason

I want the reaction to be:
“This is exaggerated, but I believe it.”

Not:
“This is a random artstation scene with a portfolio pasted on top.”

---

# 4. Cultural influence rule

The world is **Japanese-inspired**, not “look I discovered torii gates.”

I’m using Japanese influence because I genuinely like:

* the restraint
* the material discipline
* the relationship between built form and nature
* the respect for craft and finishing
* the ability to make something quiet feel deliberate and heavy

What I do **not** want is to turn this into a theme park of obvious symbols.

## Allowed influence

* cliffside roads inspired by Japanese mountain/coastal roads
* concrete retaining walls and stepped infrastructure
* restrained wood / steel / stone detailing
* layered roof silhouettes where appropriate
* narrow service architecture
* weathered signage language and spatial density inspired by Japanese infrastructure
* quiet material choices and composition
* rock gardens / drainage / carved transitions where they actually fit

## Not allowed

* random torii spam
* “samurai aesthetic” nonsense
* shrine-core Pinterest collage design
* anime cyberpunk stickerbombing
* throwing kanji everywhere for no reason
* turning the world into a tourism poster

The goal is **architectural influence**, **material discipline**, and **spatial tone**.
Not cosplay.

---

# 5. The first shipping world: macro layout

First ship gets **one controlled route** with a few major world beats.

That’s it.

Not a continent.
Not ten biomes.
Not a giant explorable map.

## First-ship route structure

### Zone 1 — Cliff Entry

Purpose:

* establish scale
* establish weather / atmosphere
* establish that this is a physical world, not a page
* give the first clean silhouette of the road system

Physical character:

* upper cliff edge
* road beginning / overlook / entry platform
* distant view of lower roads and structures
* first strong lighting statement
* minimal UI

This is where the user understands:
**I am entering something, not landing on a homepage.**

---

### Zone 2 — Descent Road

Purpose:

* teach the basic movement language
* let the user settle into the pace
* show that the route is the spine
* begin environmental storytelling

Physical character:

* winding cliff road
* retaining walls
* runoff channels
* guardrails / barriers
* a few environmental insertions
* distant structure silhouettes
* rain / mist / sea visibility depending on world state

This zone should breathe. It should not be overpacked.

---

### Zone 3 — First Interaction Overlook

Purpose:

* first meaningful content reveal
* first proof that world objects and content are actually linked
* establish the “artifact / panel / acrylic card” grammar

Physical character:

* widened platform or overlook
* one designed world object that can carry content
* camera can settle here more tightly
* enough negative space for UI overlay without clutter

This is where the user should realize:
“oh, the content is embedded into the world, not just layered on top.”

---

### Zone 4 — Cut / Tunnel / Compression Segment

Purpose:

* compress the experience
* change the rhythm
* hide the next reveal
* give a heavier, more intimate section between open vistas

Physical character:

* tunnel mouth, overhang, cliff cut, or enclosed service corridor
* less sky
* more reflected light
* tighter camera framing
* more audible / visible environmental intensity if sound exists later

This segment exists to make the next opening feel bigger.

---

### Zone 5 — Main Archive / Core Structure Zone

Purpose:

* the densest content zone of first ship
* the strongest visual identity after entry
* the place where multiple pieces of me can coexist without feeling like a list

Physical character:

* road widening into a more engineered cliffside complex
* scaffolded platforms / embedded chambers / anchored display surfaces
* a major visual centerpiece
* multiple interaction points
* the feeling that this place has been modified over time, not built all at once

This is probably where the heaviest project / skill / identity material sits.

---

### Zone 6 — Lower Outlook / Release

Purpose:

* give the user a sense of arrival
* allow a calmer close after the denser middle
* create one final image that sticks

Physical character:

* broader horizon
* ocean presence felt more strongly
* lighter content density
* a composed final perspective back toward the path traveled or outward toward the sea

The point is not “the end of the game.”
The point is **a satisfying last frame**.

---

# 6. Traversal model

## The user is moving through the world, not controlling a character

First ship is **camera-driven traversal**, not free movement.

The user scrolls.
The experience translates that into controlled movement through the route.

No WASD.
No free-fly camera.
No walking simulator control scheme.

Why:

* it protects composition
* it protects pacing
* it lets me design exact reveals
* it avoids the entire mess of “where can the player go”
* it keeps this an experience, not a game prototype

---

## Scroll maps to route progress, not raw page distance

The user should not feel like they are moving a webpage.
They should feel like they are moving through a route.

So conceptually:

* scroll input → normalized route progress
* route progress → camera rig position/orientation + environment choreography + content state opportunities

The important part is that **route progress is the real authority**, not “scrollTop but fancy.”

---

## Camera motion language

The camera should feel like a guided physical rig, not a mouse-look toy.

It should favor:

* drift
* inertia
* controlled easing
* slight banking or orientation shifts where appropriate
* strong framing at interaction points
* deliberate reveal angles

It should avoid:

* twitchiness
* over-rotation
* rollercoaster nonsense
* aggressive handheld shake
* giant FOV gimmicks

The user should feel carried, not flung around.

---

# 7. Content carriers in the world

Content does not just appear as random HTML floating over the screen.
It needs **world carriers**.

First ship should only use a small number of carrier types so the experience language stays coherent.

## 7.1 Acrylic data cards

These are one of the main reasons I want this project.

They should feel like:

* semi-physical inserts
* illuminated technical plaques
* suspended or mounted surfaces
* reflective / refractive / edge-lit objects that belong in the environment

These are not “glassmorphism cards.”
If I catch myself making dashboard glassmorphism slop, I’m doing it wrong.

They should feel like objects with thickness, mounting, lighting response, and presence.

Use them for:

* project summaries
* selected skill clusters
* key identity statements
* focused interaction moments

---

## 7.2 Embedded terminals / panels

These are fixed interfaces integrated into walls, structures, scaffolds, or equipment.

Use them when content should feel more infrastructural and less precious than an acrylic card.

Good for:

* logs
* project metadata
* system summaries
* status-like content
* links / contact surfaces if designed carefully

---

## 7.3 Markers / artifacts / anchor objects

Some content moments should begin with a **thing in the world**:

* a mounted fragment
* a lit object
* a strange mechanism
* a carved plate
* a signal marker
* a suspended assembly

The user notices the object first.
Then the camera or panel reveal makes the content legible.

That sequence matters.

---

# 8. What kinds of content belong where

I do not want the content distribution to be random.

## Route entry / early descent

Belongs here:

* identity
* immediate tone-setting
* one clean “who this is” statement
* maybe a very small number of high-signal facts

Should not be overloaded.

---

## Mid-route interaction points

Belongs here:

* skills
* selected projects
* active interests
* technical personality
* systems / graphics / simulation / frontend identity

This is where the real density can begin.

---

## Core structure zone

Belongs here:

* the strongest project material
* the “why I build like this” content
* the most memorable interactions
* the clearest evidence that I actually made the thing

This is where the experience has to cash in.

---

## Late route / lower outlook

Belongs here:

* contact
* closing tone
* maybe one final reflective statement
* outward links if they’re presented elegantly

No desperate “hire me” energy.
Just a clean finish.

---

# 9. Permanent world rules vs changeable world rules

I need a hard distinction between **what the world fundamentally is** and **what changes with state / theme / atmosphere**.

## Permanent world rules

These do not change between shipping world states:

* cliff layout
* road route
* major structures
* content anchor locations
* overall traversal path
* camera path topology
* major silhouettes
* the identity of the place

If these change per theme, I’m building two worlds and I will drown.

---

## Changeable world rules

These can change by atmosphere / theme / time state:

* sky
* rain presence / intensity
* mist density
* wetness
* surface roughness response
* water visibility / reflectivity
* lighting temperature
* cloud movement
* thunder usage
* distant visibility
* some material emphasis
* certain VFX layers

This is how I get dramatic shifts without doubling the workload.

---

# 10. First-ship object vocabulary

To stop myself from inventing random junk forever, first ship gets a constrained object vocabulary.

## Terrain / infrastructure

* cliff faces
* carved cuts
* road segments
* retaining walls
* drainage channels
* stairs where needed
* overlook platforms
* tunnel mouths / overhangs
* railings / barriers / posts

## Structures

* small cliffside pavilions or service buildings
* scaffolded attachments
* support frames
* embedded chambers / wall recesses
* utility poles / cables / mounted fixtures if tasteful

## World-content carriers

* acrylic cards
* embedded panels
* mounted markers / artifacts
* light strips / data veins only if restrained

## Atmosphere / water

* rain
* puddles
* runoff
* mist
* distant sea plane / ocean body
* occasional spray / dripping / wet sheen

If an object type does not belong to one of these groups, I need to justify why it exists.

---

# 11. Visual density rules

This project can get ugly very fast if I let every square meter scream for attention.

So:

## Dense zones

Allowed in:

* the core structure zone
* selected interaction points
* some framed vistas where layering helps scale

## Sparse zones

Required in:

* entry
* road transitions
* tunnel compression segments
* moments right before major reveals

The world needs breathing room or none of the big moments will land.

---

# 12. What I am explicitly not building in first ship

This section exists to save me from myself.

## Not in first ship

* a fully explorable city
* a combat system
* free walking
* branching route choices
* multiple cliffs / regions / maps
* complicated NPC-style world simulation
* giant lore database
* 20 different panel styles
* a million collectible artifacts
* weather seasons beyond the shipping world states
* “every hobby gets its own district”
* hyper-detailed interiors for every structure
* photoreal terrain generation rabbit holes

First ship needs one unforgettable route, not an unfinished universe.

---

# 13. The actual point of the world

The world exists to do 6 things:

1. **Make the first impression hit harder than a normal portfolio ever could**
2. **Give my content a physical home instead of a page section**
3. **Let atmosphere, weather, lighting, and motion carry emotional weight**
4. **Create a few moments people actually remember**
5. **Show engineering taste through restraint, not through dumping tech everywhere**
6. **Make it obvious that the project was built on purpose, not assembled from trendy parts**

If a world feature does not help at least one of those, it probably does not belong.

---

# 14. First-ship world test

Before I add any major world feature, I should be able to answer these:

## A. Does it strengthen the route?

If not, it’s probably noise.

## B. Does it make the place feel more real or more memorable?

If not, it’s probably decoration.

## C. Does it give content a better home?

If not, it might belong in UI instead of the world.

## D. Can I execute it at high quality without blowing up the whole schedule?

If not, it waits.

## E. If I remove it, does the world become weaker?

If the answer is “not really,” then I probably shouldn’t build it.

---

# 15. Final world sentence

If I had to explain the world in one sentence:

> It’s a cinematic coastal cliffside route built into a Japanese-inspired ruin, where the user descends through roads, cuts, overlooks, and embedded structures while the world gradually reveals my work as if it has been physically installed into the place itself.

That’s the world.
Everything else has to serve that.
